Aging is measurable, in the field.

Transistor aging slows logic over time, and faster at high temperature and voltage. Without measurement on each device, a card can drift out of specification with no warning.

September 2026Fluid Silicon4 minute read

What aging does to timing

Mechanisms such as negative bias temperature instability (NBTI) slow transistors as they are used. A path that met timing with room to spare on the day a card was installed meets it with less room every year. The rate depends on temperature, voltage and how the logic is used, so two identical boards in different racks age at very different rates.

Vendor timing models cover this by assuming the end-of-life corner from day one. That protects the worst board and wastes margin on every other one, and it still says nothing about which board is which.

One board, three temperatures

At 70 °C the projection crosses the threshold at about 3.8 years. At 55 °C it takes about 7.6 years. At 45 °C the same board is still inside its margin beyond ten years. The difference between a cool rack and a hot enclosure is the difference between a scheduled replacement and a surprise.

A model per card, not per fleet

Fluid Silicon measures every logic element about once a second for the life of the card. Each element's history is fitted to the card's own temperature record, and the fit is projected to the threshold you set. The output is a date: the predicted time-to-threshold.

  • Outliers, cards aging faster than their neighbours, are flagged early.
  • The forecast feeds replacement and capacity planning, so budgets follow measured health.
  • Where lowering the operating point buys years, the model shows it, and the change waits for your approval.
  • Every new sweep refines the fit.
