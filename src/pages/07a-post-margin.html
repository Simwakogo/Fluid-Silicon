The FPGA margin problem.

Vendors must guarantee timing for every chip they ship, so their models describe the worst one. That is the right call for a vendor. It also means every card in your fleet carries that chip's margin, for every year it runs, and here is what per-chip measurement changes.

September 2026Fluid Silicon5 minute read

Variation is a first-order design constraint

Defective and weak transistors show up as variation in the logic you build on: lookup tables (LUTs), carry blocks and interconnect. At 16, 14 and 7 nm, random variation alone exceeds 20%, on top of systematic variation across the die.

  • Random variation. Chip-to-chip and element-to-element scatter. More than 20% at 16, 14 and 7 nm.
  • Systematic variation. Smooth gradients across the die from the manufacturing process.
  • Spatial variation. Neighbouring resources that drift together, so one weak region can hold several critical paths.

These variations force conservative timing models that guarantee the slowest operating corner. Every other chip pays for that guarantee in energy and performance.

Margins are set once, for a chip that is statistically rare

Vendor timing models cover the worst die: highly defective, at elevated voltage, at high temperature and at the end of its life. Most of your chips are nowhere near that corner, yet every one of them runs as if it were. On production shells at realistic utilization we measure 30–54% of timing margin carried for that corner.*

A fleet-wide margin

Has to cover the worst chip in the whole population, including chips that never reach your racks.

A per-chip margin

Only has to cover its own measurement error, plus drift until the next measurement. Fluid Silicon measures each link to under 20 ps.

At scale

Measuring more chips finds more tails. It does not add margin to the chips that don't have them.

Silicon as a black box

Without visibility into the real characteristics of each logic component, a failure has no attribution. Teams replace boards on a schedule or after an outage. They cannot prove which cards are healthy, which are drifting or which logic caused a fault. An error in one card can take capacity offline or travel downstream before anyone notices.

Per-element measurement turns each card into evidence: what it can sustain today, how fast it is changing and when it will need attention.

What the uncertainty costs

  • Energy spent on voltage the chip does not need
  • Performance left behind a clock set for the worst die
  • Spare hardware held in reserve, just in case
  • Surprise failures that remove capacity, and errors that propagate before anyone catches them
  • Maintenance and downtime planned around guesses

What changes with measurement

Measure every chip and every element at speed, and the margin you need shrinks to measurement error plus drift until the next measurement. Power and performance is where most fleets start.
