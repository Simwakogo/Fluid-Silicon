    See what every chip can really do.

    Fluid Silicon measures the timing of every logic element on a chip while it runs. That one measurement shows what each chip can sustain, when it will need attention and how much margin it is carrying. Starting with the FPGAs you already run.

    How it works

    From one measurement to every decision.

    Today a chip is characterized once, at the factory, against the worst chip that could have been made, and then run that way for life. Fluid Silicon adds a small health layer beside your design so each chip measures its own logic, at speed, for as long as it runs.

    Measured on the chip

    Timing slack on individual LUTs, carry blocks and the links between them, while the design computes. A full sweep takes about a second; each link is resolved to under 20 ps.

    Modeled per card

    An aging curve fitted to each card's own temperature history, a time-to-threshold per element, and one health metric on every vendor.

    Tuned to what it measured

    Voltage and frequency set to what each chip can sustain, in windows you schedule, with every change re-measured.

    Repaired only when needed

    Nothing in your design moves until an element degrades toward your threshold. Then only that path moves to a healthy resource, through your change process.

    Why now

    The gap between the datasheet and the chip keeps widening.

    Setting one margin for the worst chip was the right call when nothing in the field could be measured. Three things have changed.

    > 20%

    Variation is now a first-order constraint

    At 16, 14 and 7 nm, random variation alone exceeds 20%, on top of systematic variation across the die. Two chips with the same part number are less alike with every node.

    Read more
    6+ years

    Cards stay in service longer than their models assumed

    Fleets keep cards for six years and more, and hot enclosures age faster than cool ones. Timing models are set once; the silicon keeps changing.

    Read more
    5

    Fleets are large and mixed

    One operator keeps five board generations in deployment at once, across more than a million hosts. At that scale, a margin set for the worst chip is a line item, and a surprise failure does not stay contained.

    Read more

    Where this goes

    FPGAs are where we start.

    A chip that reports its own condition is useful wherever silicon has to be trusted. FPGAs are programmable, so the health layer can be added to chips already in service, with no new silicon. That makes them the first step, not the last.

    1

    Today: FPGA fleets, read-only

    Per-element measurement on AMD and Altera parts already in production, with evaluations that start on a sample of your cards.

    2

    Next: the full loop, across industries

    Monitoring, modeling, tuning and repair on the same measurement, in data centers, at the edge and in the field, with one health metric across vendors and generations.

    Who we are

    Built by the people who did the research.

    Fluid Silicon is a Philadelphia company building FPGA health monitoring and timing measurement software. It grew out of reconfigurable-computing research at the University of Pennsylvania, which awarded founder Nhlanhla Mavuso its 2026 President's Sustainability Prize for the work.

    Nhlanhla Mavuso

    Co-Founder and CEO

    Nhlanhla led the research behind Fluid Silicon at the University of Pennsylvania, where he graduated from the Vagelos Integrated Program in Energy Research (VIPER), run jointly by Penn's School of Arts & Sciences and School of Engineering and Applied Science. Trained to look at systems by what they waste, he saw the biggest waste in deployed hardware as uncertainty: capability held back because nobody could see what each chip could really do.

    • Penn VIPER graduate
    • President's Sustainability Prize, 2026

    Dr. André DeHon

    Co-Founder and Chief Scientific Advisor

    André brings decades of reconfigurable-computing research at the University of Pennsylvania, where he is the Oliver C. Boileau Jr. and Nan Eleze Boileau Professor of Electrical Engineering. His work on FPGA architecture through Penn's Implementation of Computation Lab shaped the technical foundation behind Fluid Silicon's approach.
